Social Media, Fame, and Interacting with Fans

April 17, 2026

At times, the internet behaves a lot like an unfamiliar dog. Sometimes it sniffs, sometimes it barks, sometimes it bites. (I'm not great with dogs.) How does a creator go about their business with that dichotomy hanging over their head? How much distance should they put between them and their fans?

Some game developers take a reclusive approach, preferring to only speak about work and keep their personal lives private. IceFrog (lead developer of Dota 2) is a good example of that kind of anonymity. On the other hand, plenty of people use their Instagram or TikTok like a public scrapbook - a collage of their lives. Plenty of my peers have personal sites with work blogs.

To be blunt, attention is at a premium nowadays, and fame usually helps with that. If your goal is to amass an audience, eyes and ears are required in some capacity. If you're very, very lucky, you end up in a Lucas Pope or Toby Fox situation where your fans will show up for the things you make without the need for a constant online presence.

"Anonymity allows for protection and seclusion. It initially stems from a desire for discretion, and a lot of humility. At some point, it seemed pretentious to continue staying anonymous... it started to attract more attention than it removed.

After hiding for 20 years, the message about intention and freedom has been conveyed. It was just an artistic gesture, and anonymity was crucial during the existence of these characters, creating an oscillation between reality and fiction. It protected me, but it could also lead to a kind of isolation, which isn’t particularly pleasant." - Thomas Bangalter (paraphrased)

(This also opens up into a greater discussion of personas and alter-egos, far beyond the scope of this blog post.)

I personally draw the line when creators start fighting with fans (especially on social media). It's not the kind of environment that lends itself to a nuanced discussion where I can walk away having learned something - in fact, the extreme takes social media tends to bait out can have a lot of resentment behind them. It all feeds on drama and upset. I don't want to become bitter towards my own audience, and I've seen a lot of artists slip into that over time.

I think a lot of this hinges on two deciding factors: depends on how much you want to be famous, versus your work being famous. Some folks get away with both, but that sometimes comes at the expense of creating a public persona for oneself.

As far as I can tell, professionalism usually comes with at least a little bit of distance. That's not to say that a more accessible style of fan interaction can't come across as respectable, sincere, or serious, just that maybe that's what sometimes separates an individual from an institution. It all depends on what you're trying to make. As for the anonymity itself: privacy is easy to give away, but hard to get back. We all have to decide where that balance lies for us.
